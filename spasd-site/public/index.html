      The short version

      In January 2026, I wrote to the Sun Prairie School Board after a hard stretch in our district left a lot of students unsure what happened and who to trust. I proposed a six-part Student Safety & Trust Framework: clearer communication after serious incidents, a plain explanation of how reporting works, the same rules in every building, a real student voice, better mental health support, and regular check-ins on how it's going.

      That letter turned into meetings with district leaders, a new Student Senate at a district high school, students helping choose a principal and a superintendent, a seat on the weighted grades work group, and a proposal for a district-wide Student Advisory Council.

      The goal hasn't changed. Student voice should be a regular, documented part of how SPASD makes decisions, not something that depends on one student or one principal.

      What happened

      Two cases involving former Sun Prairie West High School staff shaped how a lot of students feel about safety and trust in our district. These are the facts as reported in public court records and the news:

      • Robert Gilkey-Meisegeier, a former coach and dean of students, pleaded guilty in federal court to producing child sexual abuse material. On April 28, 2026, he was sentenced to 18 years in federal prison. A related state case was dismissed after the federal conviction.
      • Jennifer Ploeger, a former principal, was charged with three misdemeanor counts of failing to report suspected child abuse. On June 25, 2026, she pleaded no contest to one count, the other two were dismissed, and she was placed in a deferred prosecution program. A no contest plea is not an admission of guilt, and she has not been convicted.

      Students mostly learned about both cases through rumors and the news, not from the district. That gap is a big reason this work exists.

      Sources: WKOW, Apr 28, 2026 · WTMJ, Jun 1, 2026 · WMTV15, Jun 25, 2026 · Spectrum News, Jun 25, 2026. Status as of October 20, 2026.

      Weighted grades: the Board's questions, answered

      After November 9, every question Board members ask will be listed here with the students' answer. This becomes the written FAQ that 72% of caregivers asked for. Until then, here's what families ask most:

      Does it apply to my child?
      The recommendation starts with students entering 9th grade in fall 2027. Current high schoolers keep the current scale. The Board decides.
      Why not current students?
      They already chose classes under the old rules, and changing the scale midway would shift class rank for students already being ranked.
      Which classes count?
      AP, capstone (including try-out music courses), and college-credit or certification classes that transfer to UW schools.
      Is it fair to students without access to AP?
      Weighted classes must be open to every student who meets the prerequisites, and they'll be marked in the course guide.
      When is it final?
      The goal is a Board vote on November 23. It's not guaranteed.

      Act 95, in plain language

      Wisconsin law · in effect for SPASD

      Why class rank suddenly matters a lot

      • Top 10% of your class at the end of 11th grade: guaranteed admission to every Universities of Wisconsin school except UW–Madison.
      • Top 5%: guaranteed admission to UW–Madison too.
      • Ties: if more students qualify than the cutoffs allow, SPASD breaks ties by AP and dual-credit classes first, then ACT composite score. The Board approved those rules on February 9, 2026.
      • How you find out: eligible students get an email from their high school in July, and the status is noted on their transcript.
      • You still have to apply. The guarantee covers students who complete the UW application. Schools don't send anything to UW for you, and application fees may apply.

      Why this connects to weighted grades: rank is based on GPA. On an unweighted 4.0 scale, an A in AP counts the same as any other A, so students told us they avoided harder classes or study halls to protect their rank. That's what the weighted grades plan tries to fix.

      How a Board decision gets made

      Using weighted grades as the example

      From an idea to a vote

      1. A work group builds the plan. Staff and students meet, research, and agree on a recommendation. For weighted grades, that happened October 15 and 20.
      2. A Board committee previews it. The Teaching & Learning Committee is a few Board members around a table. They ask questions early. (November 2.)
      3. The whole Board discusses it. At a Committee of the Whole, all Board members hear the plan and ask questions, but don't vote. (November 9, with students presenting.)
      4. The Board votes at a regular meeting. If the plan is ready, it's put on a regular meeting agenda for approval. (Goal: November 23, not guaranteed.)
      5. The district puts it in place. For weighted grades, that means marking classes in the course guide in December.

      💡 A state law says phones stay away during class time, with exceptions for emergencies, health needs, IEP/504 plans, and when a teacher says it's OK.

      The phone law, explained

      2025 Wisconsin Act 42 · in effect since July 1, 2026

      What changed and why

      • What: every Wisconsin public school district had to adopt a policy limiting students' personal phones, tablets, laptops and gaming devices during instructional time. School-issued devices don't count.
      • Exceptions every policy must allow: emergencies or a perceived threat, managing a health need, following an IEP or 504 plan, and when a teacher approves it for learning.
      • Districts can go further: the law is a minimum. Each district decides the details, like storage and consequences, in its own policy.
      • How it happened: the bill passed the Legislature with support from both parties, and Governor Evers signed it on October 31, 2025. Wisconsin joined more than 35 states with similar limits.

      Where the money comes from

      The SPASD budget, simplified

      Why districts talk about deficits

      • Revenue limits: Wisconsin caps how much each district can collect from state aid and property taxes combined. When the cap doesn't keep up with costs, districts face gaps.
      • Referendums: to go over the cap, a district has to ask voters. Sun Prairie voters approved an operating referendum in 2022 ($9 million a year when fully phased in, 60% yes), another operating referendum on November 5, 2024, and building projects plus operating money in April 2019.
      • Enrollment: funding follows students, so when enrollment drops, so does revenue.
      • Scale: SPASD serves about 8,400 students. The 2025–26 budget was reported at about $221 million. That's very roughly $26,000 per student per year, or about $146 per student per school day. Rough math, not an official figure.

      2. What gets cut if it fails?
      3. How much would it cost a typical homeowner?
      4. Is it one-time or recurring?
      5. How will the district report back on how it was spent?

      Public records, 101

      Wisconsin public records law

      You can ask for documents too

      Wisconsin's public records law lets anyone request records from government bodies, including school districts: policies, budgets, contracts, and many emails. You don't have to say why. Some records, like private student information, are protected.

      How: email the district's records custodian with a specific request, for example:

      Under Wisconsin's public records law, I request a copy of the district's current policy on student personal electronic devices, and any student-facing summary of it. Electronic copies are fine. Thank you.

      Start small and specific. Fees can apply for large requests.

      Glossary

      Words that come up a lot on this site, in plain language.

      Act 95
      A 2023 Wisconsin law that guarantees admission to Universities of Wisconsin schools for students in the top 10% of their high school class, and to UW–Madison for the top 5%. Since rank depends on GPA, how a district weights grades matters.
      Weighted grades / 5.0 scale
      Harder classes count for more in your GPA. On a 5.0 scale, an A in a weighted class counts as 5.0 instead of 4.0.
      Capstone class
      An advanced, top-level class in a pathway that builds on earlier courses.
      AP (Advanced Placement)
      College-level classes with a national exam that can earn college credit.
      Dual credit
      A class that earns high school and college credit at the same time.
      Board of Education
      The elected group that sets district policy, approves the budget, and hires the superintendent.
      Teaching & Learning Committee
      A smaller Board committee that previews academic changes before the full Board votes.
      Student Senate
      An elected group of students at one high school that meets with the principal.
      Student Advisory Council
      A proposed district-wide group of students from every middle and high school who would meet regularly with district leaders.
      "You said, we did"
      A public update showing what students asked for and what the district did about it.
      Climate survey
      A survey about how safe, respected, and heard people feel at school.
      Title IX
      A federal law that bans sex discrimination in schools, including sexual harassment.
      Mandated reporter
      Someone, like a teacher or principal, who is required by Wisconsin law to report suspected child abuse or neglect.
      No contest plea
      When a person charged with a crime doesn't fight the charge but doesn't admit guilt either.
      Deferred prosecution
      A deal where a criminal case is put on hold. If the person meets set conditions, the charge can be dismissed.
      Revenue limit
      A state cap on how much a school district can raise from state aid and local property taxes combined.
      Referendum
      A vote by local residents, for example to let a district spend above its revenue limit.
      ALPR (license plate reader)
      Cameras that automatically read and record license plates. Sun Prairie police are switching from Flock to Axon cameras.

      How a school referendum works

      1. There's a cap. Wisconsin limits how much money a district can collect from state aid and local property taxes combined. That's called the revenue limit.
      2. To go over it, voters decide. A district can ask voters to approve going over the cap (for things like staff and programs) or to borrow money (for buildings).
      3. The Board puts it on the ballot. The School Board votes to ask the question, and it appears on an election ballot.
      4. Voters say yes or no. If it passes, the district can collect or borrow that money. If it fails, the district works within the cap.

      Sun Prairie example: a $25 million referendum passed in 2024. About $7 million went to staff pay after a wage study.

      Source: How the Budget Works (district finance meeting, Aug 18, 2026).

      Student rights cheat sheet

      This is general information, not legal advice. District policy decides the details at your school. A staff review of this section has been requested.

      • Speech: students keep free speech rights at school, unless speech seriously disrupts school or falls under other limits the courts allow (Tinker v. Des Moines, 1969).
      • Searches: school staff need "reasonable suspicion" to search a student, a lower bar than police need (New Jersey v. T.L.O., 1985).
      • Records: parents can see and ask to correct your school records. Those rights move to you at 18 (a federal law called FERPA).
      • Phones: Wisconsin now requires every district to limit personal devices during class time, with exceptions like emergencies and health needs.
      • Board meetings: anyone, including students, can speak during public comment.

      Read SPASD's actual rules in Board policies on BoardDocs, or ask your counselor.

        Report #4 · October 2026

        From heard to “here's what changed”

        What students learned from district leaders on October 20, 2026 about incident communication, the first “you said, we did” commitment, and what student voice should look like by June 2027.

        At a glance

        • Commitment: district newsletters launching in November will go to all students, not just families.
        • Incident communication: what students hear depends on direct impact, age, and severity. Some details can't be shared by law.
        • Shared goal: by June 2027, students should be able to name something that changed because they spoke up.
        • What's next: a wider, more representative survey on what good student voice looks and sounds like.

        Who we met

        Dr. Michael D. Morgan Jr., Director of Systemic Equity & Inclusion, and Marlita Bevenue, Director of Strategic Communications. We ran out of time before discussing a district student advisory council and equity questions, and plan to pick those up at a follow-up meeting.

        How incident communication works today

        Safety comes first, facts come second, and the message comes after that. Dr. Morgan noted his answers reflected his own understanding, not a formal written district protocol.

        What decides whether students are told: direct impact (an incident at one school is unlikely to be shared at a school it didn't affect), the age of students, and severity (more serious incidents get more communication and come from higher up).

        The steps, as Ms. Bevenue described them:

        1. Make sure everyone is safe and accounted for.
        2. Communications works with the principal and district leadership to confirm facts.
        3. Decide who is affected, what each audience needs, and what's ready to share.
        4. Her team prepares and reviews the message.
        5. It comes from the principal, district leadership, or the superintendent, based on severity.
        6. More information follows as it's confirmed.
        What can't be sharedWhy
        Names of students involvedStudent privacy laws; many involve minors
        Individual consequences and disciplineProtected student records
        Grades and other student recordsProtected student records
        Personnel mattersEmployee protections; directors often don't have the details either

        Both directors stressed accuracy. Rumors on social media can spread faster than the district can verify facts, and sending wrong information would hurt trust more than a short delay.

        What students asked for

        • Short, factual messages. Even with no names, “a medical incident closed this area, here's what you need to know” takes five minutes and stops rumors.
        • Name the law. When something can't be shared because of a law, say which one. Many students will look it up.
        • Trust students' understanding. Students often feel information is held back because adults assume they won't get it. Most do.
        • “You said, we did” updates. Follow up on what actually happened, even if it's “nothing changed yet, here's what's happening.”

        Ms. Bevenue agreed students should hear directly from the district so they have a reliable source: clarify what's known, correct inaccurate information, and explain what's still being learned.

        How the SPASD Budget Works

        From a meeting with the district's Director of Business & Finance on August 18, 2026

        The short version: the district's money mostly pays for people, the amount it gets depends on how many students enroll, and Wisconsin's per-student funding hasn't kept up with inflation.

        TopicWhat I learned
        Biggest costAbout 80% of spending is staff. The rest covers busing, supplies, and utilities.
        RevenueDriven by enrollment through state formulas. Revenue must equal expenses.
        Budget calendarBuilt over 8–9 months, starting in February, adopted in October.
        Long-term planA 5-year forecast is updated every February.
        2024 referendum$25 million passed; about $7 million went to staff pay after a wage study.
        SavingsFund balance is about 21%. Around 15–20% is considered healthy.
        Federal Title grantsAbout $160,000 less than last year. These fund the highest-need schools.
        Special educationThe state covers roughly 30% of actual cost; general funds cover the rest.

        The most common misunderstanding

        Many people assume more state aid means more money for schools. Under Wisconsin's revenue limit, the district can only collect a set amount, so more state aid mostly lowers the local property tax levy instead of raising the total.

        Biggest risks ahead

        • Per-student funding not tied to inflation
        • Enrollment projected to decline, partly due to housing costs
        • Possible operating deficit down the road as costs rise

        Weighted Grades: What Other Districts Do

        From the SPASD weighted grades student work group

        SPASD uses an unweighted 4.0 GPA, where an A counts the same in every class. The work group is looking at how nearby Wisconsin districts handle weighting.

        DistrictWhat gets weightedHow
        WauwatosaAP and UW–Green Bay dual credit5.0 scale. Chosen because they're open to all students, taught by district teachers, overseen by outside groups, and earn college credit. Not retroactive.
        ElmbrookAP, level 5 world language, select advanced classes, Early College Credit, Start College NowSmall bonus on a 4.0 scale per semester. No official class rank.
        MiddletonAP only5.0 scale. Chose the smallest change possible; open to more after 1–2 years of data.

        Where SPASD's group stands

        • Agreed on a 5.0 scale instead of bonus points
        • Drafted criteria on Oct 15 and agreed on a rollout on Oct 20 (below)
        • Surveyed students (903 responses) and staff; surveyed caregivers in English and Spanish (531 responses in the first week, below)
        • Also researching Verona and Oregon
        • Goal: a framework to the Board by the end of November 2026

        The recommendation (drafted Oct 15, confirmed Oct 20)

        The criteria team started from one idea: weighted grades should recognize course rigor, because grades should reflect challenge, effort, and talent, and should account for how many prerequisites a class has.

        Would be weighted (5.0 scale)Would not be weighted
        Capstone classes, including try-out based music courses (repeatable)College-credit or certification classes that are equivalent to courses or elective credit already offered at the high school
        AP coursesClasses that don't transfer to UW colleges and universities for credit
        Classes that earn industry certification or college credit

        Why the exceptions: some Madison College classes, like Start College Now or a PE class taken to meet a graduation requirement, only count at Madison College or match what the high school already offers at the same rigor. A college class that transfers to UW schools for credit still counts, even if the high school has a similar class. For example, Calculus 2 at Madison College would be weighted.

        Who it applies to

        On October 20 the students in the work group agreed weighted grades should start with incoming freshmen in 2027–28 (the Class of 2031) and roll up from there. Current students already chose their classes based on the old system, and many followed counselors' advice to balance their schedules, so changing the rules partway through wouldn't be fair. Students in the group said that even when they'd personally benefit.

        What students said (Act 95 survey, Dec 2025 – Jan 2026)

        903 students answered the district's Act 95 survey before the January 26 Board hearing.

        District criteria, with credits moved down9%

        • 55% had never heard of Act 95 or didn't know the details before the survey. Only 16% said they were very familiar.
        • Students mostly heard about it from SP30 (46%) and from friends (35%). District email reached 19% and school newsletters 16%.
        • Many wrote that the current rules made them avoid study halls or question taking AP classes, because credits counted toward rank.

        531 parents and caregivers answered in the first week: 263 with students at Sun Prairie West, 245 at Sun Prairie East, and 204 with only elementary or middle schoolers.

        Proposal · Spring 2026

        Student Senate Restart Framework (Phase I)

        A plan I wrote for relaunching a high school Student Senate as a small pilot before growing it into a full body.

        The restart puts sustainability, consistency, achievable goals, and a real partnership with administration ahead of size. During Phase I, the Senate works as a small advisory team rather than a full legislative body.

        Core objectives

        • Re-establish consistent student–administration dialogue
        • Create repeatable, documented meeting systems
        • Complete one measurable improvement initiative
        • Build student awareness and trust in the Senate
        • Represent different grade levels and involvement areas
        • Review and respond to student concerns in a documented way

        A measurable initiative needs four things: a clearly defined problem, documented student input, a written recommendation, and a tracked outcome or response from administration.

        Authority and scope

        The Senate gives formal advice on academic policy (grading, weighted GPA, scheduling), climate and equity, school improvement planning, discipline framework feedback, resources that affect students, and facilities. It doesn't override administration, but it should be consulted on major student-impacting changes before they're final.

        Structure

        • Phase I: a founding team of 4–6 students chosen by short application and interview, from at least two grade levels, for one semester, followed by an evaluation
        • Phase II: if Phase I works, more grade-level seats, small committees, and 8–12 members
        • Roles: President, Vice President, Secretary (publishes minutes), and Communications Lead (public updates)

        How it runs

        • Meets every two weeks. Agendas go out 24–48 hours ahead, and minutes within 72 hours.
        • A majority is a quorum. Recommendations pass by simple majority, and framework changes need two-thirds plus administrative approval.
        • Proposals move from issue, to meeting review, to draft, to a written brief for administration. Everything is archived.
        • A short public summary goes out after any formal administrative review.
        • A faculty advisor keeps it running and handles logistics but doesn't control votes.

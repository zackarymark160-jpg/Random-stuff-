Executive Summary

Sun Prairie Police have used Flock Safety license plate reader (ALPR) cameras since December 2023 — nine cameras today, six owned by the department and three donated by Woodmans. The department credits the system with stolen-vehicle recoveries and help in missing-person, shooting, and homicide cases, and says it follows written Policy F-18 (30-day retention, documented searches, alert verification, audits, and no federal sharing without a court order or the Chief's approval).

The city will not renew Flock (six cameras end Dec. 31, 2026; three end Mar. 31, 2027) and plans to move to Axon Outpost cameras in 2027. Chief Kevin Warych briefed the Public Safety Committee on October 7, 2026 on the Axon Outpost transition; this was a staff report, not a vote, and no contract has been approved yet. A non-binding Axon budgetary estimate obtained since that meeting puts the proposed 9-camera system at roughly $235,389 over a 10-year term (May 2027–April 2037, about $23,539/year) — details in Section 7. The change follows a wave of Wisconsin communities dropping or restricting Flock.

Update, Oct. 16: the department has since released a written FAQ on the Axon transition. It says the city will own the data, that sharing will be limited to approved state and local agencies under formal agreements (no federal access, and no agreements with agencies in the federal 287(g) program), and that every search requires an offense category and reason and is logged in a record that users and administrators cannot delete. It also says the final retention period for Axon has not been set. These are department statements, not contract language, and the FAQ does not address Fusus, livestreaming, or what happens to the old Flock data. See Section 10.

Open questions residents are raising: cost versus benefit, how success is measured, audit transparency, data-sharing scope, contract control, and whether the program could expand without public approval. Notably, Axon markets Outpost as combining plate reading with live video streaming and vehicle-attribute recognition, unified through its Fusus platform — the budgetary estimate obtained so far only itemizes ALPR/"Vehicle Intelligence" hardware and software, with no Fusus or livestreaming line item, but that is not confirmation those capabilities are excluded from the final contract. Section 14 explains how to take part.

1. Overview & Camera Network

Sun Prairie Police Department (SPPD) has used squad-mounted plate readers since roughly 2009 and a fixed Flock Safety camera network since December 4, 2023. Cameras photograph passing plates and vehicles and check them against agency-authorized "hotlists" (stolen vehicles, wanted persons, AMBER Alerts). Trained personnel can also search stored reads for documented investigations.

The network has 9 cameras: 6 owned by SPPD (term ends December 31, 2026) and 3 donated by Woodmans (term ends March 31, 2027).

3. How the System Has Been Used

From SPPD's internal tracking of notable outcomes (December 2023 – September 2026):

Stolen vehicle recoveries

Dozens of hits leading to recovered vehicles, several tied to weapons or drug seizures, and one possible link to a Madison homicide investigation (May 2024).

Missing persons & homicide cases

In a January 2025 case, a missing person's vehicle was captured before and after her disappearance; her body was later found near Flock cameras and a suspect was charged with first-degree intentional homicide. A September 2026 Sun Prairie shooting and a March 2026 Madison attempted homicide were also aided by camera hits.

Retail theft, fraud & robbery

Vehicles identified in shoplifting, credit-card fraud, an armed robbery (Sept. 2025), and a $79,000 ATM "jackpotting" case (Dec. 2025).

Hit-and-runs & fleeing vehicles

Vehicles identified when witnesses gave partial or incorrect plates, including a fatal pedestrian hit-and-run in Green County (Mar. 2026).

Mutual aid

Shared reads assisted Madison, Deforest, Middleton, Verona, Cottage Grove, Dane County, and Green County.

Warrants, domestic cases & threats

Used to locate wanted subjects, domestic-violence suspects, and people who threatened others.

Not every hit ends in an arrest: records also show outdated "stolen" flags, vehicles already recovered, welfare checks, and civil matters. These outcomes come from the department's own summaries and were not independently verified.

Updated log: through September 21, 2026

SPPD's "Flock Utilization & Hits" log, updated through Sept. 21, 2026, adds 25 new notable items since the last update on May 22, 2026. Recent additions include stolen-vehicle hits (June–Sept. 2026), a Sept. 7 attempted-homicide case in Madison where the vehicle hit a Sun Prairie camera, a Sept. 9 Sun Prairie shooting with one suspect taken into custody, and several retail-theft and warrant cases. A handwritten note on the copy reviewed reads "only outcomes": the log lists notable outcomes, not every hit.

What the log does and doesn't show: because it lists only notable outcomes, there is no total of how many alerts were wrong or went nowhere, so a success rate can't be calculated from it. The log itself, though, includes cases where the system's output didn't pan out: a "stolen" flag the originating agency never removed (Dec. 2023); a vehicle ruled out as the suspect vehicle in a fatal hit-and-run (Mar. 2026); an owner who entered the wrong plate as stolen (May 2026); and several stolen-vehicle alerts that turned out to be civil disputes or vehicles the owner had already recovered. Asking for error and false-alert counts is a reasonable request.

4. National Standards: The 13 Principles

At a September 23, 2026 Senate Judiciary Subcommittee hearing, national law enforcement groups (including the International Association of Chiefs of Police, the National Sheriffs' Organization, the National District Attorneys Association, and the National Fusion Center Association) released 13 recommended ALPR principles. SPPD states it follows all 13.

  • Written policies on use, access, retention, sharing, and audits
  • Permitted uses limited to trained, authorized personnel for legitimate purposes
  • Authorized access, removed when no longer needed
  • Auditing & accountability, with discipline up to termination for misuse
  • Required training for anyone with access
  • Documented queries tied to a case number or public-safety purpose
  • Verification of plate matches before enforcement action
  • Confidentiality of ALPR data from public disclosure
  • Meaningful retention balancing investigations and privacy
  • Responsible data sharing, with no selling of data
  • Equal restrictions on access to non-governmental ALPR data
  • Data security requirements for anyone storing ALPR data

The summary sheet provided names 12 of the principles; the 13th was not on the pages reviewed.

5. Local Policy & Oversight (Policy F-18)

SPPD Policy F-18, Automatic License Plate Readers, was last updated March 3, 2026 (first adopted 2010). Key provisions:

  • Background checks: CIB and FBI fingerprint checks before anyone gets access.
  • Alert verification: officers must visually confirm the plate and verify vehicle status (NCIC/TIME) before enforcement action, and note this in reports.
  • Documented searches: every search needs a case number, a standardized incident category, a stated reason, and a lawful objective.
  • Retention: 30 days unless needed for an investigation.
  • Auditing: a System Administrator compiles periodic audit reports (reads, alerts, deployments, violations); frequency is set in a separate procedure approved by the Chief. Meeting notes indicate audits roughly monthly to weekly.
  • Case type and number required: meeting notes say that since about November 2025 every search must carry a case type, case number, and reason. The same notes mention an internal investigation involving misuse; the details were not captured, so that is worth asking about directly.
  • How audits work: notes describe audits done monthly (often weekly) with help from the department's records-management system, which flags triggers such as the same plate being searched repeatedly; history is archived. A handwritten note from the July 10 briefing appears to record that no misuse had been found in the prior year. Confirm this with the department before relying on it.
  • Camera locations: notes say SPPD picks the locations, with an "equity lens," consistency across the city, and cameras strictly on roadways.
  • Privacy safeguards: logged, restricted access; no surveillance without reasonable suspicion of a crime; no monitoring by race, religion, gender, or political affiliation; annual public transparency reports.
  • What is captured: plates and vehicles only — no facial recognition, and no detection of people, gender, or race.
  • Prohibited uses: immigration enforcement, traffic enforcement, harassment, use based solely on a protected class, and personal use.

6. Data Sharing & Federal Access

  • Data may be shared through the department-approved network with designated law enforcement agencies — about 200+ agencies within roughly a 50-mile radius, per the portal and meeting notes.
  • No sharing with federal agencies unless required by court order or expressly approved by the Chief.
  • Release to non-law-enforcement parties is prohibited without a court order, subpoena, or the Chief's approval; public-records requests go through the department's open-records process.
  • SPPD states its data is owned by the department and never sold.
  • 287(g) and sharing partners: notes from the Oct. 7 meeting jot down sharing partners that participate in the restricted 287(g) program with ICE, with a figure of "13–14" next to it; the notes don't make clear exactly what that number counts. The department's Oct. 16 FAQ says that under Axon it will prohibit data-sharing agreements with agencies participating in 287(g). That implies the restriction is new, so it is fair to ask how many current Flock sharing partners would be cut off, and which ones.
  • Under Axon: notes say sharing would run through a formal MOU (agreement with protections) with each agency, and that while Flock is by far the most common system among nearby agencies, Axon's ALPR network is not large. That could mean fewer neighbors to share with.

Contract terms residents have asked about

  • Hardware ownership: Flock owns the physical cameras; the city cannot move or alter them without Flock, and relocation carries a reinstall fee.
  • Camera placement: planned jointly, but Flock has final say and can veto a location.
  • Data ownership vs. Flock's access: the city owns its data and Flock may not sell it, but the contract lets Flock itself disclose footage to law enforcement or government when legally required or in good faith for legal process, security, fraud, or emergencies — separate from SPPD's own access rules.
  • Anonymized data: Flock may use de-identified data to improve its products and train machine-learning models.
  • Liability: capped at fees paid in the prior 12 months; Flock disclaims responsibility for "repeat criminal activity" or failure to capture footage.
  • Exit: the city may terminate for non-appropriation of funds on 30 days' notice; either side may end the agreement over an uncured material breach.

More fine print from the 2023 Flock agreement

Additional pages of the signed agreement and order form have since been reviewed. Several terms are standard vendor boilerplate, but residents asking "what exactly did the city agree to?" may want to know about them:

  • Permitted purpose: the service is licensed "solely for the awareness, prevention, and prosecution of crime, bona fide investigations and evidence gathering for law enforcement purposes." Flock may suspend service if it believes the system is used for anything else.
  • Who can log in: only the city's officers, employees, or agents, using city-issued email addresses; the city is responsible for everything its users do. Footage lives with a third-party cloud host, and the contract says Flock may use third parties for hosting and cellular service.
  • Retention is Flock's default, not a legal floor: Flock deletes footage on a rolling 30-day basis, and the city is responsible for downloading and archiving anything it needs to keep longer.
  • Audio and video language: the agreement's opening recitals describe Flock's platform as capable of capturing "audio, video, image, and recording data," and the definition of Flock Services includes "audio detection." The order form itself lists only the Falcon plate-reader camera, and meeting notes indicate live audio is a Flock capability that SPPD does not use.
  • Network features listed on the order form: state and nationwide license-plate lookup across cameras that have opted in, search by "vehicle fingerprint" (make, color, stickers, roof racks), search by full, partial, or temporary plates, unlimited custom hotlists, and real-time NCIC alerts. The packet does not say which of these SPPD has switched on.
  • Fees: Flock may change fees for a renewal term with 60 days' notice; unpaid balances can accrue 1% per month, and Flock can suspend service on an undisputed invoice more than 30 days overdue (after 30 days' written notice).
  • Support and outages: Flock promises "commercially reasonable" efforts to answer support requests within 72 hours and to repair or replace defective hardware within 7 business days of inspection. Services are provided "as is"; for outages caused by third-party services or maintenance, the city's remedy is extra days added to the term, not a refund.
  • Hardware and installs: the city may not move or tamper with cameras, or the warranty is void and the agreement can be terminated for breach. If cameras are lost or damaged, replacement is at a posted reinstall fee. Flock can remove the hardware if the city misses payments.
  • Indemnity and insurance: Flock indemnifies the city for patent/copyright claims and for property damage or injury directly caused by its installation, capped at the prior 12 months of fees. Flock must carry general liability ($1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate), umbrella ($10M), professional liability/E&O ($5M), and commercial auto ($1M) coverage, plus cyber liability ($5M).
  • "Morality" clause: if the customer or its agents become the subject of an indictment, contempt, scandal, or similar event that would "negatively impact or tarnish Flock's reputation," Flock may terminate on written notice.
  • Publicity and feedback: Flock may use the city's name and trademarks in its marketing, and any suggestions or feedback from the city or its users are assigned to Flock, including intellectual-property rights.
  • Assignment: either side may assign the agreement without the other's consent to an affiliate or to a buyer or successor in a merger or sale of the business.
  • Governing law: the law and courts of the state where the customer is located (Wisconsin). The agreement also says a purchase is not contingent on any promised future features.

9. Open Questions & Concerns

Questions prepared for a July 10, 2026 briefing with Chief Warych, and concerns residents have raised, include:

Vendor selection & success measures: Were alternatives considered before Flock, and how is success measured beyond individual case stories?
Legal review & scope: What Fourth Amendment review was done, and could the system expand without public approval? Courts have generally not found ALPR collection itself illegal, but "mission creep" is a common worry. Chief Warych's Oct. 7, 2026 memo to the Public Safety Committee cited a specific body of case law on this point: Wisconsin's City of Horicon v. Albert (1999) and State v. Folkman (2013); Seventh Circuit decisions United States v. Tuggle (2021), United States v. House (2024), and United States v. Hammond (2021), plus a pending Seventh Circuit ALPR appeal, Scholl v. Illinois State Police (argued Sept. 29, 2026); U.S. Supreme Court precedents United States v. Knotts (1983), United States v. Jones (2012), and Carpenter v. United States (2018); and two 2026 ALPR-specific rulings from other circuits, United States v. Porter (5th Cir.) and Schmidt v. City of Norfolk (E.D. Va.). All of these describe ALPR or similar vehicle-tracking technology as generally not constituting a Fourth Amendment "search" in the cited circumstances — none of them is a ruling on Sun Prairie's program specifically, and Scholl remains pending.
Misuse & stalking risk: Policy calls for investigation of suspected misuse; some residents want to know whether any violations have been found. Notes mention both a mandatory case-type/case-number requirement since Nov. 2025 and an internal investigation involving misuse, and a July 10 note that appears to say no misuse was found in the prior year; the department can clarify how those fit together. Another open item from the notes: with Flock's automated system, the same plate could be wrongly flagged multiple times, so how will Axon's audits and automatic flagging catch errors?
Cost vs. benefit: Support is strongest for stolen-vehicle recovery and missing-person cases; is the cost proportionate? A non-binding Axon estimate obtained after Oct. 7 puts the proposed system at about $235,389 over 10 years (roughly $23,500/year) — higher than the current Flock cost of about $15,000–$18,000/year, though the Axon figure also bundles two mid-contract hardware refreshes Flock's model didn't include. See Section 7.
Data sharing: With 200+ agencies able to search 30 days of history, residents want clearer public accounting of who has looked at Sun Prairie's data and why.

10. The Move to Axon

Sun Prairie's plan

  • The city will not renew Flock. Police will present an Axon Outpost contract to the Public Safety Committee on Oct. 7.
  • City officials say the city would own and control its ALPR data, including access and export, and that Axon would consolidate data storage, body-worn cameras, and TASER devices on one platform. Because that platform includes TASERs and body cameras, the vendor relationship is broader than ALPR alone.
  • Retention: Flock's is 30 days now (7 days from Jan. 1, 2027 per notes); Axon's is discussed at 15–21 days.
  • Axon's own materials describe agency-owned data, tamper-evident audit trails, no automatic federal access, plates-only capture (no facial recognition), and planned tools for flagging unusual searches. These are vendor statements; the contract language is what binds the city.

What the department's Oct. 16 FAQ says

In reply to follow-up questions the author sent the Chief on Oct. 6, SPPD provided a written FAQ, Automated License Plate Reader (ALPR) Technology: Frequently Asked Questions, signed by Chief Warych. (The full text is on the Resources page of the companion website.) Its main statements about the planned Axon system:

  • Ownership: the City owns and controls the data; Axon acquires no ownership rights and cannot sell it to insurers, businesses, or anyone else. The City pays a per-camera fee that includes storage.
  • Retention: 30 days today. The Axon retention period "has not yet been established"; the department is evaluating a shorter one, after which data is deleted automatically.
  • If the contract ends: the City has 90 days to retrieve its data, after which it is deleted, and can export its audit and activity reports.
  • Who has access: SPPD sworn personnel and dispatchers by role, plus approved state or local agencies under a formal agreement. Axon employees have no ongoing access; temporary access for tech support must be approved by the department, limited in scope and time, logged, and auditable. Security includes role-based access, multi-factor authentication, CJIS compliance, and audit logs.
  • Searches: each search requires an offense category (from a list the department authorizes) and a specific reason. If a user picks an unauthorized category, the system returns no data. Every search is logged, including by administrators, and neither users nor administrators can delete or edit the log.
  • Sharing: only with approved state and local agencies that sign a Memorandum of Understanding; "no blanket sharing." No federal access, and no agreements with agencies in the 287(g) program. Axon's "federated sharing" lets an agency share only data it owns, so partners can't pass Sun Prairie's data along. An agency that violates its agreement loses access. Legal requests to Axon for the City's data are directed to the City.
  • Out-of-state use: asked whether another agency could use Sun Prairie data for an investigation outside Wisconsin, the FAQ says "potentially, yes," if the search meets the agreement's terms, and that the department does not intend to allow use of its data to investigate conduct that is lawful in Wisconsin but prohibited elsewhere.
  • What the cameras collect: plate image, a wider vehicle image, basic visible characteristics (color, make, type), and date, time, and location. No facial recognition, no speed detection (and none planned), no automatic owner lookup, no profiles of owners, and no continuous live tracking.
  • Effect on crime: the FAQ says individual successes "do not independently establish that ALPR technology reduces overall crime rates."

What the FAQ doesn't say. It does not mention Fusus or livestreaming (the Axon capabilities raised in Section 11), whether the old Flock data will be transferred, exported, or discarded, whether Axon may use de-identified city data for product development or AI training, how often audits happen or who reviews them, or whether Policy F-18 will be rewritten for the new system. It also describes intentions ("will establish," "intends to") rather than signed terms, so the Axon contract and the MOUs are what will actually bind the city. The Chief's reply also pointed to the recording of the Oct. 7 committee meeting for further answers.

Source: SPPD, ALPR Technology FAQ, provided to the author by email on Oct. 16, 2026.

About Axon Enterprise

Axon Enterprise, Inc. (NASDAQ: AXON) is based in Scottsdale, Arizona. Founded in 1993 as a Taser maker (TASER International until renamed Axon in 2017), it now sells Tasers, body cameras, in-car cameras, digital evidence and records software, and, through its Fusus acquisition, real-time crime center and ALPR technology. It has roughly 4,000+ employees, several billion dollars in annual revenue, and a market value in the tens of billions.

National context: Axon stock and the ICE Taser contract

Separately from Sun Prairie's decision, financial disclosures show an account in President Donald Trump's name bought $1–5 million of Axon stock on February 10, 2026. Two weeks later, on February 24, 2026, U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) posted a federal contracting-database notice seeking information from vendors for a large Taser purchase.

Details reported on the ICE contract itself:

  • Scale: ICE sought about 17,800 new Tasers — up from roughly 4,300 then in the field — specifically T10 models with 10-probe cartridges effective up to 45 feet, plus unlimited cartridges and agent training.
  • Value and term: Roughly $220 million over five years.
  • Vendor: The notice's technical specifications reportedly matched only Axon's products; Axon is the sole maker of conductive-energy weapons meeting ICE's stated specs. Vendors had until March 13, 2026 to respond, and as of this report it was not publicly confirmed that Axon had been formally awarded the contract.
  • Stated justification: ICE said the devices give agents "greater distance to try to de-escalate a hostile situation" and a better chance of a successful probe connection.
  • Criticism: Sen. Adam Schiff criticized the spending amid broader growth in ICE's contracting; Diane Goldstein of the Law Enforcement Action Partnership questioned whether Tasers would actually be used to de-escalate and noted the risk of electrical-shock injury.

No charges have been filed and no insider trading has been proven; there is no public evidence the president was involved in the procurement or that Axon knew of the purchase. The White House says the holdings are managed by third parties and denies any conflict. Ethics experts cited in coverage say the concern is the appearance of a conflict, not proven misconduct. This involves a separate federal (ICE) procurement for Tasers — not ALPR cameras, and not Sun Prairie's contract — but residents may consider it relevant context when asking about the vendor the city is choosing for a different product line.

Sources: Times of San Diego (Mar. 3, 2026); 24/7 Wall St. (June 29, 2026); CNBC (June 29, 2026); NOTUS.

11. Axon Outpost: Beyond Traditional ALPR

Axon's own marketing describes Outpost as more than a plate-reader replacement. Axon says Outpost combines three capabilities in one fixed device: automated license plate recognition, live video streaming, and vehicle-attribute recognition (make, model, color, and features like roof racks, bumper stickers, or missing plates). A companion product, Axon Lightpost, adds the same three capabilities to existing streetlights. Axon says both feed into Axon Fusus, a real-time operations platform that can unify Outpost/Lightpost data with other connected cameras and sensors — Axon's own materials describe this as bringing "visibility across every fixed, mobile, and community-sourced ALPR-enabled camera."

That means the relevant question for Sun Prairie isn't only "is Flock being replaced by another plate reader?" It's which of these capabilities the city is actually purchasing and enabling, and which rules will govern them:

  • Will livestreaming and vehicle-attribute recognition be turned on, or only plate reading?
  • If vehicle attributes are collected, are they searchable without a known plate, and how long are they retained?
  • Is Axon Fusus included in the proposed contract, and if so, what other cameras or data sources could it connect to later?
  • Does SPPD's Policy F-18 — written for plate reads — automatically cover livestreaming and attribute recognition, or would it need to be updated?
  • What approval (council vote, budget process, policy amendment) would be required before additional cameras or capabilities are added later?

Update: A non-binding Axon budgetary estimate obtained after the Oct. 7, 2026 committee meeting itemizes only ALPR camera hardware and an "Axon Vehicle Intelligence – ALPR License" for all 9 cameras — it does not list Fusus or a livestreaming license as separate line items. That leans toward "plate reading only" for the configuration priced so far, but a budgetary estimate isn't the final statement of work, and Outpost's base camera hardware may be capable of more than what's separately licensed. See Section 7 for the full cost breakdown. The department's Oct. 16 FAQ (Section 10) says the cameras capture basic vehicle characteristics (color, make, type) but is silent on livestreaming and Fusus, so those questions remain open.

Sources: Axon product pages for Outpost, Lightpost, and Fusus (axon.com); Axon's 2026 press materials on its expanded ALPR camera lineup; Axon Quote Q-932192-001 (Sept. 22, 2026).

12. Flock & Axon: A Complicated History

Because Sun Prairie is leaving one vendor for the other, it's worth knowing the two companies have a tangled relationship:

  • 2020: Axon made a strategic investment in Flock and the companies partnered, offering Flock's fixed ALPR alongside Axon's Fleet in-car systems.
  • Ongoing (pre-2025): Flock's Vehicle Fingerprint technology was integrated into Axon Fleet 3, letting Flock-derived evidence flow automatically into Axon Evidence.
  • August 28, 2024: the companies agreed to a modestly expanded integration involving Fusus and Flock devices.
  • Early-mid 2025: Flock says Axon unilaterally decided to end the API partnership, telling Flock that continued openness was "no longer in Axon's best interests."
  • Since July 24, 2025: per Flock, Axon no longer supports new mutual customers, though existing customers may still get some support — Flock says the practical effect on customers is minimal since it opened its own APIs at no cost.

Flock's partner directory still listed an "Axon (Evidence.com)" integration as of this report, which is worth reconciling directly with both companies rather than assuming either description is current. Because Sun Prairie is moving to Axon's own Outpost hardware (not continuing to run Flock alongside Axon), this history mostly matters for one practical question: will Sun Prairie's historical Flock data be transferred, exported, or simply left behind when the Flock cameras come down.

Sources: Flock Safety, "Axon Plans to Sever APIs With Flock" (June 2025); Axon's 2020 partnership announcement.

16. Plain-English FAQ

Does this use facial recognition?

No. SPPD and Flock both state the system reads plates and vehicles only, not faces, gender, or race.

Can ICE or other federal agencies see Sun Prairie's data?

Under current policy, no — not without a court order or the Chief's specific approval. The department's Oct. 16 FAQ says the same will hold under Axon: no federal access, and no sharing agreements with agencies in the 287(g) immigration program. Whether that is written into the Axon contract and the sharing agreements is still worth checking.

Does the city own the cameras?

Currently, no — Flock owns the hardware even though SPPD paid for most of it and Woodman's donated three units. Whether Sun Prairie or Axon will own the new cameras hasn't been confirmed.

How long is my car's plate data kept?

30 days under the current policy and contract, unless it's tied to an active investigation. Flock has said it's moving toward 7 days; Axon's retention was discussed at 15–21 days in earlier notes, but the department's Oct. 16 FAQ says the Axon period has not been established yet.

Is this legal?

Courts have generally not found ALPR data collection itself illegal. That doesn't resolve every question residents have raised about retention length, scope, or oversight — those are policy questions, not settled legal ones.

Could the city add more cameras or capabilities later without another public vote?

That depends on the contract language, which is one of the biggest open questions in this report (see Section 13).

What does this cost?

The 2023 Flock contract ran about $15,000–$18,000 a year depending on which figures you use. A non-binding Axon budgetary estimate obtained after the Oct. 7 meeting puts the proposed 9-camera Outpost system at about $235,389 total over a 10-year term (2027–2037), or roughly $23,500/year — but it's explicitly marked a draft, non-binding estimate, not a signed contract, so the final number could change.

Who owns the plate data, and can it be sold?

The department says the City owns it and that Axon can't sell it to insurers or other businesses. If the contract ends, the City says it has 90 days to retrieve its data before it is deleted. The signed contract's wording is what counts.

Can I find out if my car's plate has been read?

Not directly — ALPR data is treated as confidential, and routine public-records requests for it are typically denied. You can ask the department directly about its process.

Who do I talk to if I have more questions?

See Section 14, "How to Weigh In," for city contacts, or reach the author of this report directly.

Preguntas frecuentes

¿Usa reconocimiento facial? No. El sistema solo lee placas y vehículos, no rostros.

¿Puede el gobierno federal (como ICE) ver los datos? Bajo la política actual, no, sin una orden judicial o aprobación específica del Jefe de Policía. Las preguntas frecuentes del departamento (16 de octubre de 2026) dicen que con Axon no habrá acceso federal y que no se permitirán acuerdos de intercambio con agencias del programa federal 287(g). Estas son declaraciones de intención del departamento, no cláusulas de un contrato.

¿De quién son los datos de las placas? El departamento dice que los datos pertenecen a la ciudad, que no se pueden vender, y que cada búsqueda se registra con una categoría de delito y un motivo, y esos registros no se pueden borrar. También son declaraciones de intención, no cláusulas de un contrato.

¿Es esto legal? Los tribunales generalmente no han determinado que la recolección de datos ALPR sea ilegal, pero persisten preguntas sobre políticas y supervisión.

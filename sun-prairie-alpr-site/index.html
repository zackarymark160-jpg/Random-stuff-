      The short version

      The Sun Prairie Police Department has used Flock Safety license plate reader cameras since December 2023. There are 9 cameras: 6 owned by the department and 3 donated by Woodman's. The city has said it won't renew with Flock and plans to move to Axon Outpost cameras, with a switch planned for May 2027.

      On October 7, 2026 the Chief gave the Public Safety Committee a report on the plan. It was a report, not a vote, and no contract has been approved. On October 16 the department sent a written FAQ with promises about data ownership, search logging and sharing. Those are statements of intent, not contract terms, and several questions are still open.

      This site collects the documents, puts them in order, and shows which answers are confirmed and which aren't.

      How a plate reader works

      Show this section
      1. CameraMounted on a pole. It photographs passing vehicles.
      2. ReadSoftware reads the plate and notes the time, place, and the vehicle's color, make and type.
      3. MatchThe plate is checked against hotlists, such as stolen vehicles. A match is an alert, not proof of a crime.
      4. KeepReads are stored for a set time. Today that is 30 days. The Chief says Axon would start at 14 days for a three-month trial.
      5. SearchOfficers can search the stored data. The department says each search is logged with a reason.

      No facial recognition: the department and Flock both say the system reads plates and vehicles only. Terms like retention and hotlist are explained on the FAQ tab.

      Answered

      Are Fusus and livestreaming included?

      The Chief’s Oct. 27 email says there will be no livestreaming: the city did not pay for it and he does not want it. His reply did not separately address Fusus. This is a department statement, not a contract term.

      Partly

      Can federal agencies access the data?

      SPPD's Oct. 16 FAQ says no federal access, and no sharing agreements with agencies in the federal 287(g) program. Axon's materials say there is no built-in federal access. Whether that is written into the contract and the sharing agreements isn't public.

      Partly

      Who owns the data and cameras?

      The FAQ says the city owns the data and Axon can't sell it. If the contract ends, the city has 90 days to retrieve it before deletion. Who owns the cameras, and the actual contract wording, haven't been seen.

      Partly

      How long is data kept?

      30 days now. In his Oct. 27 email the Chief says Axon retention will start at 14 days for a three-month trial. His staff will check whether any investigations are affected; if not it stays at 14, and if it is a problem it moves to 21. The Chief decides, not the Council. Not yet in writing.

      Partly

      How are searches limited and checked?

      The FAQ says every search needs an offense category and a reason, and logs cannot be edited or deleted. The Chief says a Detective Sergeant reviews searches monthly, possibly more often. That is a stated practice, not yet a written policy.

      Open

      Where will the cameras go?

      No location plan has been published.

      Partly

      What happens to historical Flock data?

      The Chief says it is deleted after 30 days once the Flock cameras come out. Audit logs are the exception: they are kept for transparency, and he says he needs to follow up on that one.

      Partly

      Which outside agencies can search Sun Prairie's data?

      About 200 agencies within roughly 50 miles can today. Under Axon the FAQ says only approved state and local agencies with a signed agreement. The list of partners isn't published.

      A 3-minute comment you can adapt

      Public comment is usually short, so a few clear points beat a long speech. Fill in the brackets, change anything that isn't true for you, and use your own words. Reading it aloud takes about two minutes.

      Hello, my name is [NAME] and I live at [STREET OR NEIGHBORHOOD] in Sun Prairie.

      I'm here about the plan to switch the police department's license plate readers from Flock to Axon. I'm [for / against / undecided about] the plan, and I have three questions I hope the committee will get answered in public before anything is signed.

      First, retention. The Chief says Axon retention would start at 14 days for a three-month trial, then stay at 14 or move to 21. Today it is 30 days. Please put the final number in writing, and say who can change it.

      Second, the contract. The department has said there will be no federal access and no sharing with agencies in the 287(g) program. Those promises are in a FAQ, not a contract. Will they be written into the agreement, and will the full contract be published?

      Third, what's included. The draft estimate lists plate readers only. Will livestreaming or Fusus be turned on? And what approval would adding cameras later need?

      Thank you for your time. I'd appreciate a written answer, and my email is [EMAIL].

      What the comparison suggests

      Sharing is the common flashpoint

      Dane County and Fitchburg both pointed to how many outside agencies could see local data. Milwaukee responded by cutting direct outside searching. Sun Prairie's department says Axon sharing will be limited to signed agreements, which is the thing to get in writing.

      Audits vary a lot

      Waukesha Police moved to monthly audits and Milwaukee announced audits every six months. Sun Prairie's Chief says a sergeant reviews searches monthly, which would match Waukesha, but it isn't in a written policy yet.

      Some Milwaukee council members want 7 days. Sun Prairie keeps 30 today. The Chief says Axon would start at 14 days for a three-month trial, then 14 or 21.

      Not everyone left

      Milwaukee and Green Bay kept Flock with new limits. The question in most places was how much oversight comes with the cameras, not only whether to have them.

      Flock and Axon, in plain words

      What is the same

      Both companies sell cameras that read license plates and log the plate, the time and the place. In both cases the real rules, such as how long data is kept, who can search it and who it is shared with, come from the contract and the department’s policy, not from the brand name.

      What is reported to differ

      Axon advertises plate-reading cameras that attach to existing streetlamps, and it also reads plates from cameras inside patrol cars (404 Media). Sun Prairie’s Flock cameras are on utility poles (WMTV 15). Whether any of that changes what residents should worry about depends on the contract.

      The questions that matter are the same for either vendor: how long the data is kept, who can see it, how searches are checked, and whether the rules are written into a contract. See Updates for where each answer stands.

      Plain-English answers

      Short answers to the questions people ask most. Department statements are marked as statements, not contract terms.

      Does it use facial recognition?

      The department and Flock both say the system reads plates and vehicles only, not faces. That is a statement, not a contract term, so it is fair to ask for it in writing.

      Can federal agencies get the data?

      The department’s Oct. 16 written FAQ says there will be no federal access and that sharing is limited to approved state and local agencies under signed agreements. The Chief says a public MOU on data sharing will be written after a contract is signed. Until then it is a statement of intent.

      How long is the data kept?

      Old Flock data is deleted after 30 days. The Chief said Axon would start at 14 days for a three-month trial, then stay at 14 or move to 21. He decides that, and it is not in a contract I have seen.

      Who can search it, and how is that checked?

      Officers can search the stored data. The department says every search is logged with an offense category and a reason, and the log can’t be deleted. The Chief says a Detective Sergeant reviews searches monthly.

      Does a match mean someone committed a crime?

      No. A match against a hotlist is an alert, not proof. An officer still has to decide what it means.

      Will anything be livestreamed?

      The Chief says there will be no livestreaming and that he does not want it. The Axon estimate has no separate line for Fusus or livestreaming, and Fusus was not separately addressed in his answers, so that is still worth asking about.

      Is a plate reader tracking me personally?

      It is built to read plates, not faces, but it does log the car’s plate with the time and place. If you ride in a family car, that car’s plate is logged like any other trip. A plate is tied to a person only when someone looks it up in another record.

      No contract has been approved, and no ALPR vote date has been posted. Final city budget approval is Nov. 17, and it is an open question whether that vote includes the Axon contract. The Updates tab lists the open questions.

      Glossary

      ALPR (automated license plate reader)
      A camera system that photographs passing vehicles and uses software to read the license plate. It also records the time, place and often details like color, make and type of vehicle, and checks plates against lists.
      Flock Safety
      The company that supplies Sun Prairie's current plate reader cameras under a contract signed in October 2023.
      Axon
      The company the department plans to switch to, with a planned transition in May 2027. Axon also makes Tasers and body cameras.
      Outpost / Lightpost
      Axon's plate reader camera products. See the report for which one the department is considering and what it costs.
      Fusus
      A real-time camera and data platform owned by Axon. The department's FAQ does not say whether Fusus or livestreaming would be part of the plan, which is one of the open questions on the tracker.
      Hit
      When the camera reads a plate that matches a list, such as a stolen vehicle or a wanted person. A hit is an alert, not proof of a crime, so officers are supposed to confirm it.
      Retention
      How long the plate data is kept before it is deleted. Under the Flock contract the data is deleted on a 30-day rolling basis. The department says the Axon retention period has not been set yet.
      Audit log
      A record of who searched the system, when, and why. The department says every search will need an offense category and a reason, and that logs cannot be deleted.
      Sharing / federated sharing
      Letting other agencies search your cameras' data. The department says sharing with Axon will be limited to approved agencies under signed agreements, with no federal access.
      MOU (memorandum of understanding)

      Thirteen real cases

      Open one at a time. These are drawn from news reports, and some accounts are disputed. Where they are, the card says so and does not pick a side.

      Court Norfolk, Virginia: a judge says nearly 200 cameras are legal

      What happened

      Two Hampton Roads residents, represented by the Institute for Justice, sued the city. They argued that Norfolk’s network of nearly 200 Flock cameras violates the Fourth Amendment. In late January 2026 a federal judge sided with the city. He said other courts have almost uniformly found that photographing a plate on a public road, and keeping and searching a database of those photos, is not a warrantless search. He also noted that the technology is evolving and the balance could change. The plaintiffs said they planned to appeal. Check for newer news, because that was reported before any appeal.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      “Is it legal?” and “is it a good idea?” are different questions. A court can say a program is allowed while a city still chooses to set tighter limits than the law requires.

      Talk about it

      If a court says plate readers are legal, who should decide what limits Sun Prairie sets, and how?

      Source: WTKR, Jan. 27, 2026

      Audit Illinois: a state audit asks who the data was shared with

      What happened

      A 2023 Illinois law bars plate reader operators from sharing data to enforce immigration or abortion laws. The Secretary of State’s office audited Flock after the Texas search (next card) and alleged that Flock gave U.S. Customs and Border Protection access to Illinois data through a pilot program, and that Flock had no safeguards against out-of-state police breaking the law. Flock disagreed with the legal claims. Its CEO wrote, “We clearly communicated poorly,” and said Flock had paused all pilots with federal agencies. The article notes the Illinois law has no specific penalties.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      A rule against sharing only matters if someone checks that it is followed. An audit is how a promise becomes something you can verify.

      Talk about it

      Who would check that Sun Prairie’s data-sharing promises are being kept, and how would the public find out?

      Source: Capitol News Illinois via NPR Illinois, Aug. 27, 2025

      Search log Texas: one search, two accounts of why

      What happened

      The Electronic Frontier Foundation reported that in May 2025 a Texas sheriff’s office searched Flock networks across the country for a woman who had an abortion. The reason typed into the search log read “had an abortion, search for female.” The sheriff said deputies were checking on her welfare because her family feared she would bleed to death, and that she was never under investigation. Flock called the claim that the search was about the abortion “unequivocally false” and said she was searched for as a missing person. EFF said court records showed a “death investigation.” These accounts disagree, and this page does not decide between them.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      The person searching types the reason, and that reason is what an audit later reads. A log is only as useful as the rules about what a reason must say and who reviews it.

      Talk about it

      What should a search reason have to include, and who should read them?

      Source: EFF, Oct. 7, 2025

      Contract Evanston: ending a contract is not the same as the cameras leaving

      What happened

      Evanston ended its Flock contract in August 2025, citing the Illinois audit and Flock’s admission about the federal pilot, deactivated its cameras, and issued a termination notice effective Sept. 26, 2025. Flock’s chief legal officer called it a “communication error” that has been fixed. A month later, according to Ban The Cams! (an advocacy group summarizing the Evanston RoundTable), Flock reinstalled cameras without permission and the city sent a cease-and-desist letter. The same source says Flock was challenging the termination and that the city faced potential liability of $145,500.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      The exit terms of a contract decide what happens when a city wants out: who removes the cameras, how fast, and who pays.

      Talk about it

      What should Sun Prairie’s contract say about removing cameras and deleting data if the city ends it?

      Source: ABC 7 Chicago, Aug. 29, 2025 · Ban The Cams!, Sept. 25, 2025 (an advocacy group summarizing the Evanston RoundTable)

      Pilot Austin: a pilot that was almost extended without end

      What happened

      In June 2025 Austin’s city manager pulled a proposal to extend the police plate reader pilot indefinitely, after negative public reaction. Dozens of residents spoke against it, and opponents raised concerns about Flock’s contract allowing data sharing with private sources and federal agencies. Police leaders said the cameras helped solve dozens of cases and were used properly. Police use of Flock’s fixed cameras, and of plate readers in more than 500 police vehicles under separate Axon contracts, was to be shut off at the end of June 2025, and the Flock extension could return to the council later.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      A “pilot” can quietly turn into a permanent program. Sun Prairie’s Axon plan starts with a three-month trial, so the question of what happens at the end of it matters.

      Talk about it

      What would count as a successful three-month trial, and who decides whether it continues?

      Source: Community Impact, June 4, 2025

      Law Virginia: the state sets a 21-day limit

      What happened

      Virginia’s 2025 law caps how long law enforcement can keep plate reader data at 21 days. The governor had recommended 30 days, but House lawmakers rejected that change 47 to 46, and he signed the bill as passed. State Police use of readers on state highways, bridges and tunnels needed the General Assembly’s approval again in the 2026 session.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      When a state sets a rule, every city has the same floor. As far as I can tell Wisconsin does not have a state plate reader law yet. Its attorney general has called for one. See the Wisconsin section below.

      Talk about it

      Should retention be decided by the state, by each city, or by each contract?

      Source: Land Line, Aug. 8, 2025

      Council Oshkosh: the council changed its mind in a day

      What happened

      Oshkosh’s council backed Flock and then reversed itself the next day. The police chief said officials were misled about what the system could do and that he could no longer recommend it. This is the account on the Compare tab.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      Votes can move fast when new information comes out. Residents who ask questions before a vote can change what the council knows.

      Talk about it

      What would you want the council to know before it votes on a contract?

      Source: Compare tab

      Removal Henrico County, Virginia: the county pulled the cameras itself

      What happened

      Henrico ended its Flock contract over data-collection, data-access and privacy concerns. Flock was given until Oct. 21 to remove the cameras and did not, so county crews took down more than 120 of them on Oct. 31. Flock says it would have complied and is coordinating the rest of the removal.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      Who owns the poles, the cameras and the data after a contract ends is a question to settle before signing.

      Talk about it

      If Flock or Axon missed a deadline in Sun Prairie, what would the contract let the city do?

      Source: WTVR, Nov. 2, 2026

      Contract Cedar Rapids: a 164-page contract and a decade-long plan

      What happened

      Cedar Rapids is phasing out more than 70 Flock cameras and using Axon plate readers. The contract runs 164 pages and budgets for 20 Axon Outpost cameras. Data is held for 30 days unless it is part of an active investigation. KCRG cites a $14 million agreement with Axon announced in August and a $14.6 million public safety investment the council approved in June for a decade-long contract.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      Most of the rules live in the contract, and the contract is long. Reading it, or at least asking for the key terms in writing, is part of knowing what a city agreed to.

      Talk about it

      Which five pages of a 164-page contract would you read first, and why?

      Source: KCRG, Nov. 2, 2026

      Ballot York, Maine: voters decide directly

      What happened

      York put a question on the ballot asking whether to ban automatic license plate readers, including Flock’s. Voters approved it, and the town must remove or shut off its Flock cameras and end the contract. Police said the cameras helped in investigations, and residents raised privacy concerns. The article does not give the vote count.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      Some places let voters decide a question directly. Wisconsin cities can ask voters an advisory question, which tells officials what residents think but does not bind them.

      Talk about it

      Should a plate reader decision go to voters, or to the council? What are the tradeoffs?

      Source: Fox 23 Maine, Nov. 4, 2026

      Ordinance Bloomington, Indiana: a unanimous ban, then the mayor

      What happened

      Bloomington’s council voted unanimously to ban plate readers and shared third-party cameras. The ordinance goes to the mayor, and a two-thirds council vote could override a veto. Park closed-circuit cameras and police body cameras are exempt. The city’s 11 plate-reader cameras were already removed, and local police said Flock cameras had helped solve crimes.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      An ordinance is a local law with steps: committee, council vote, mayor. Knowing the steps tells you when your voice counts most.

      Talk about it

      At which step would you contact an alder in Sun Prairie, and what would you ask?

      Source: WFIU, Oct. 15, 2026

      Claims A claim to test: “it would have helped in a homicide”

      What happened

      Sun Prairie’s Assistant Chief called the cameras “an immediate force multiplier” and said cameras along the east corridor would have helped investigators in an October 2024 homicide. That is his claim in a news interview.

      Why it matters in Sun Prairie

      Law, rights and how the city works

      The Fourth Amendment

      It protects against unreasonable searches. In Carpenter v. United States (2018), the U.S. Supreme Court held that getting someone’s historical cell-phone location records is generally a search that needs a warrant. In January 2026, a federal judge in Norfolk, Virginia ruled that plate readers there did not violate the Fourth Amendment, though he noted the technology is changing and the plaintiffs said they would appeal. Carpenter on Oyez · Norfolk ruling

      Wisconsin’s public records law

      You can ask in person, by phone or in writing. You generally do not have to say who you are or why you want the record. A request has to describe the subject or time period reasonably. The government must respond “as soon as practicable and without delay”; the state Department of Justice has said about 10 working days is generally reasonable for a simple request. If you ask in writing and are denied, the denial must be in writing with specific reasons. Only records that already exist are covered. Wisconsin DOJ guide

      Wisconsin’s open meetings law

      Public bodies have to give public notice of their meetings and what will be discussed, generally at least 24 hours ahead, with shorter notice allowed in some circumstances. So the agenda is the best clue to whether something can be voted on. Check Wis. Stat. 19.84 for the details. The statute

      How the city works

      Roughly: residents elect a mayor and alders (the City Council). Committees, like the Public Safety Committee, hear reports and make recommendations. The Chief runs the police department. The council approves the budget, which is why Nov. 17 matters. Check the city’s website for the current structure.

      Vendor vs. contract

      A company can say a lot in a blog post or a press release. A contract is what binds it. Flock’s CEO wrote after the Illinois audit that the company “clearly communicated poorly” and had not built separate permissions for federal users. The lesson: ask what the contract requires, not what the website promises.

      The cost, in plain arithmetic

      The draft Axon estimate is $235,389 over 10 years. That is about $23,539 a year, or about $64 a day. Spread over nine cameras, it is about $2,615 per camera per year. This is arithmetic on a non-binding draft, not a comparison with the city budget.

      Five questions for any tool that collects data about people

      School cameras, body cams, drones, gunshot detection, an app, a database. The same five questions work for all of them.

      1. What exactly does it collect?
      2. How long is it kept, and where is that written?
      3. Who can see or search it, and is every search logged?
      4. Who is it shared with, and on what terms?
      5. What happens if something goes wrong, or if we want out?

      Resumen

      El Departamento de Policía de Sun Prairie (SPPD) ha usado cámaras lectoras automáticas de placas (ALPR) de la empresa Flock Safety desde diciembre de 2023. Actualmente hay 9 cámaras: 6 propiedad del departamento y 3 donadas por Woodman's. La ciudad no renovará el contrato con Flock y planea cambiar a cámaras Axon Outpost en 2027. El 7 de octubre de 2026, el Jefe Kevin Warych presentó un informe (no una votación) sobre la transición a Axon ante el Comité de Seguridad Pública; aún no se ha aprobado ningún contrato. Un presupuesto preliminar y no vinculante de Axon, obtenido después de la reunión, estima el costo total en aproximadamente $235,389 dólares durante 10 años (2027–2037) para las 9 cámaras.

      Preguntas frecuentes

      ¿Usa reconocimiento facial? No. El sistema solo lee placas y vehículos, no rostros.

      ¿Puede el gobierno federal (como ICE) ver los datos? Bajo la política actual, no, sin una orden judicial o aprobación específica del Jefe de Policía. Las preguntas frecuentes del departamento (16 de octubre de 2026) dicen que con Axon no habrá acceso federal y que no se permitirán acuerdos de intercambio con agencias del programa federal 287(g). Estas son declaraciones de intención del departamento, no cláusulas de un contrato.

      ¿Cuánto tiempo se guardan los datos? 30 días bajo la política y el contrato actuales con Flock, salvo que estén vinculados a una investigación activa. El departamento dice que el período con Axon todavía no se ha fijado.

      ¿De quién son los datos de las placas? El departamento dice que los datos pertenecen a la ciudad, que no se pueden vender, y que cada búsqueda se registra con una categoría de delito y un motivo, y esos registros no se pueden borrar. También son declaraciones de intención, no cláusulas de un contrato.

      ¿Es esto legal? Los tribunales generalmente no han determinado que la recolección de datos ALPR sea ilegal, pero persisten preguntas sobre políticas y supervisión.

      What is this?

      Some cameras read license plates. The police use them in Sun Prairie. The cameras are called ALPR.

      What do they do?

      A camera takes a picture of a plate. A computer reads the letters and numbers. It checks the list of stolen cars and wanted plates. If it finds a match, it sends an alert to police.

      What is happening now?

      Sun Prairie has 9 cameras from a company called Flock. The city plans to switch to a company called Axon in May 2027. The city has not signed a contract yet.

      What do we know?

      Flock keeps plate data for 30 days now. The Chief says Axon would keep it for 14 days at first. The Chief says there will be no livestreaming. A police sergeant checks searches each month.

      What do we not know?

      Those are promises. They are not in a contract yet. We have not seen the contract. We do not know if the rules will be written down.
